A factory stamps the serial numbers in numbers onto one big crate by multiplying them all together and

writing the product in base base (digits 0 to base - 1, so base 10 is ordinary decimal).

Return how many zero digits the written product ends with. The product can have tens of thousands of

digits, so it must be reasoned about without building it with ordinary numbers.

The product is 252000, which ends in three zeros in decimal. The numbers factor as 40 = 2^3

* 5, 75 = 3 * 5^2, 12 = 2^2 * 3 and 7, so the product holds

five 2s and three 5s. A decimal zero uses one 2 and one 5, and the 5s run

out after three.

The product is 1944. Since 12 = 2^2 * 3, each trailing zero in base 12 needs two

2s and one 3. The product has three 2s and five 3s, so only one zero can be

formed: 1944 is written as 1 1 6 0 in base 12.

A trailing zero in base b consumes one copy of b, i.e. e copies of each prime p

with p^e exactly dividing b. The scarcest prime sets the limit.

Factor the base by trial division. For each prime p with exponent e in the base, sum the

exponent of p in every number, then take floor(sum / e). Return the minimum over the primes of

the base.

The product is divisible by base^k exactly when, for every prime power p^e in the base, the product

has at least k * e copies of p. So the answer is the minimum

over primes of floor(copies / e). Factoring the base costs at most about 31,623 trial divisions, and counting

copies over 5,000 numbers is cheap, so nothing larger than a 32-bit integer is ever created.

10 = 2 * 5, so both primes need e = 1.

The numbers 40, 75, 12 and 7 contain five 2s and three 5s, giving allowances of 5 and

3, so the answer is 3.

O(sqrt(base) + n * (log max + distinct primes of base)). O(1)
