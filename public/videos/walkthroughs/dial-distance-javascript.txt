A combination lock has a dial with 2^bits settings, each shown as a bits-bit number. The dial is

turned one click at a time from click 0 up to click 2^bits - 1, and after the

last click it wraps around to click 0. The setting displayed at click k is k XOR (k

>> 1), that is, k XOR-ed with k shifted right by one bit. For every pair [a, b]

of settings, return the fewest clicks needed to turn the dial from setting a to setting b, turning

in whichever direction is shorter.

Setting 6 shows at click 4 and setting 9 at click 14, so they are 10 clicks apart

one way and 22 the other way around the dial, and the answer is 10.

Setting 17 shows at click 30 and setting 3 at click 2, so they are 28 clicks apart

one way and 4 the other way around the dial, and the answer is 4.

A one-bit dial has clicks 0 and 1 showing settings 0

and 1, one click apart. A setting is zero clicks from itself.

Each click bit is the XOR of all setting bits at or above it, so folding the setting

onto itself with shifts recovers the click.

Compute click = setting ^ (setting >> 1), then ^= click >> 2, >> 4, >> 8, >>

16. Take apart = |clickA - clickB| and return min(apart, 2^bits - apart) for every pair.

The encoder XORs each bit with its upper neighbour, so the decoder must XOR each bit with everything

above it, which is a prefix XOR from the top. Shifting by 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16

and XOR-ing builds that prefix in five operations for a 31-bit value. Since the clicks form a cycle,

the answer is the shorter of the two arcs between the click numbers.

For bits = 5, setting 6 decodes to click 4 and setting 9 decodes to click 14.

The arcs are 10 and 22, so the answer is 10.

O(pairs) with a constant five folding steps per setting. O(pairs) for the output
